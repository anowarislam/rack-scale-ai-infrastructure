# Manager or director capstone: align commitments with capacity and evidence

Own one portfolio choice for Meridian: what to promise now, what to defer, and which investment or operating change to fund next. Your audience is an executive or service owner who needs a concrete decision and its consequences, not a tour of technical details. Maintain enough technical depth to challenge the capacity and recovery assumptions.

## Start with the constraint and the time horizon

Separate installed supply, qualified usable supply, and a commitment under the required failure scenario. Separate money available from delivery time, qualification capacity, and specialist time. A purchase can be economically attractive and still irrelevant to the immediate gap. A repair improvement can be promising without being bankable capacity.

Keep scenario assumptions visible. If there is no measured probability for a disruption, evaluate concrete failure cases rather than inventing a probability to rank options. If stakeholders can choose different service commitments, show the consequence of each choice.

## Worked exemplar fragments

**Capacity arithmetic, synthetic:** "With four 32-GPU domains, loss of one leaves 96 GPUs. Twenty percent remaining headroom and 8-GPU jobs permit 72 GPUs of commitment. Adding a fifth equal domain would leave 128 after one loss and permit `floor(128*.8/8)*8 = 96`. It meets the requested count in that model after delivery and qualification. It does not address two simultaneous domain losses, topology restrictions, or this week's shortage."

**Near-term decision:** "I will hold the existing justified commitment while seeking a time-bounded reduction in deferrable demand. The purchase decision is evaluated on the six-week horizon. The two-week launch does not depend on unqualified hardware or an unreplicated repair pilot. If product agrees to defer only part of the gap, the remaining unmet demand is reported explicitly."

**Team capacity:** "Twelve discretionary hours cannot cover an eight-hour intervention plus a six-hour compatibility test. I choose the compatibility test because it gates the readiness decision, then allocate six hours to the intervention. Its remaining work is explicitly deferred. If the reviewer cannot be booked, I revisit that sequencing rather than leave the team idle under a nominal priority."

**Risk statement:** "The residual risk is that demand exceeds the reduced commitment or another failure occurs outside the modeled single-loss case. The trigger for a new review is either revised demand, a qualified domain returning, or a failed recovery check. The owner of the service commitment retains the decision; the infrastructure team supplies current evidence."

These are examples, not the answer to every variation. A different choice can pass if it fits the stated constraints and has stronger evidence.

## Produce a decision that can be reviewed

Submit a one-page recommendation supported by a transparent worksheet. Include the near-term and later time horizons, capacity under named losses, the bottleneck in delivery or qualification, explicit deferred work, owner/decision rights, and reversal triggers. Use ranges or scenarios for uncertain inputs; do not disguise assumptions as forecasts.

Run the Month 23 two-session process. After the repair pilot or demand agreement changes, revise the worksheet and the recommendation. Show one actual course artifact you used to validate the technical argument. Demonstrate follow-through by reviewing the delegated evidence, closing a criterion, and retaining unresolved risk.

For people leadership, include one proposed operating mechanism, such as a weekly qualified-capacity review with explicit exceptions and owner actions. Explain the behavior it is meant to change and what evidence would show benefit. This is a transfer design. It becomes workplace-evidenced only after sustained real use and measured outcomes.

## Assessment and remediation

The reviewer checks whether the recommendation answers a concrete decision, separates time horizons, respects technical and team constraints, exposes residual risk, and changes when evidence changes. A large budget does not justify an infeasible schedule. A staffing request without a measured bottleneck is insufficient.

If the case is weak, collect the missing capacity or work-flow evidence and redo the choice. Do not add invented return-on-investment percentages. The capstone demonstrates bounded course-observed judgment; it does not establish longitudinal management, hiring, succession, or director-level experience. Procurement, staffing changes, and real external communications remain outside the exercise.
